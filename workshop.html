2 · Meet the PPG

6 min

PPG (photoplethysmography) is the green light on the back of a smartwatch. Every heartbeat pushes a pulse of blood through your wrist. More blood absorbs more light, so the sensor sees a wave: one bump = one heartbeat.

We loaded 10 seconds of Sam's PPG while sitting on the couch. It's stored in a list called ppg_rest, with 25 readings every second. Each reading is one number: how much light bounced back at that moment. The red dots on the plots show where the first 5 readings sit on the wave.

  • Count the tall peaks in the 10-second plot by eye. Ignore the small bump after each one (that's the heart valve closing).
  • That's beats in 10 seconds. Multiply by 6 to get beats per minute.

Now plot ppg_exercise (Sam jogging) and ppg_seizure (recorded during a seizure). Edit the cell below.

Think: could you tell exercise and a seizure apart from 10 seconds of PPG alone? Both are fast. Keep that question in mind.

3 · Teach the computer to count beats

8 min

A watch can't count by eye. We need a rule. A reading is a heartbeat peak if it is:

  • bigger than the reading just before it, and
  • bigger than the reading just after it, and
  • above a threshold (so the small bumps don't count).

The threshold is a cut-off height: a peak only counts as a heartbeat if it is taller than this number. The for loop below checks every reading one by one.

Clue: look at the numbers on the y-axis of Sam's resting plot in section 2. The small bumps never go above about 0.5. The tall peaks are higher, but in the jogging and seizure recordings some only reach about 0.7. So pick a number between 0.5 and 0.7: taller than every small bump, shorter than every tall peak. Type it in place of ___ on the first line, then run the cell.

Stuck?
Any number from 0.55 to 0.7 works, for example PEAK_THRESHOLD = 0.6. Check the plot: every tall peak should have a red circle, no small bump should, and the dashed line should sit between them. Python is fussy: spaces at the start of a line matter, and so do colons : at the end of def, for and if.

Did the computer agree with your count? Now that you have built a way to detect these peaks, we will use this function on the other two recordings (seizure and exercise) to detect Exercise (or Seizure) heart rate peaks which we will multiply by 6 to get beats per minute. Try for both variables.

  • Experiment: change PEAK_THRESHOLD to 0.3 and run that cell again. What goes wrong? Why do we need a threshold? (Change it back afterwards.)

Problem: exercise and seizure both give a heart rate above 120 bpm. A single snapshot can't tell them apart. Doctors look at how the heart rate changes over time.

4 · Exercise vs seizure over time

6 min

The watch runs your beat counter all day and saves one heart-rate reading every 10 seconds. Here are two 10-minute events from Sam's week that a doctor has already labelled:

  • event_jog: Sam jogging to school

The highest heart rates are almost the same. Look at the shape of each line. Zoom in on the moment each one starts by printing a slice of the list (readings 16 to 24 cover minutes 2.7 to 4):

The clue: during exercise the heart speeds up gradually as muscles need more oxygen. In many seizures the brain's electrical storm hits the nerves that control the heart, so the rate shoots up within seconds, even if the person is lying still.
